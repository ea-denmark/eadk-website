## In 10 seconds

Effective altruism is about making the world better by using reason, research and critical thinking to create as much positive impact as possible.

Effective altruism is both a philosophy and a global movement of people helping each other make the biggest possible difference with their time and their money.

## Four thoughts you probably already agree with

If you nod along to the four points below, you are closer to effective altruism than you might think.

{{< values >}}
{{< value title="1. Helping others matters" >}}
When people are in need and we can help, we should. Most people think millionaires ought to give back, but few realise that even an ordinary Danish salary puts you among the richest 5% in the world.

[See where you sit on the global income scale](https://giveffektivt.dk/hvor-rig-er-jeg)
{{< /value >}}
{{< value title="2. Everyone has equal moral worth" >}}
Everyone has an equal claim to a happy, healthy, meaningful and free life – regardless of where they live, how rich they are, or their ethnicity, age, gender, disability or beliefs.
{{< /value >}}
{{< value title="3. Helping more is better than helping fewer" >}}
All else being equal, we should save more lives, help people live longer, and make more people happier. If the same resources can improve 20 lives instead of one, it is better to improve the 20.
{{< /value >}}
{{< value title="4. Our resources are limited" >}}
We only have so much time and money. Spending it on one thing means we cannot spend it on something else.
{{< /value >}}

## How do you choose which problems to work on?

How do you choose which problems to tackle when so many of them seem both serious and important? Researchers in effective altruism have developed a framework to help. It says you should weigh three things:

{{< values class="guidance-values" >}}
{{< value title="Big" >}}
Some problems affect only a few people, others affect billions. The bigger the problem, the more good we can do by solving it. Ask yourself how many people would benefit from progress in this area. Ten people? A thousand? A million?

Solving ten percent of a big problem is better than solving ten percent of a small one.
{{< /value >}}
{{< value title="Neglected" >}}
Is the area overlooked or undervalued by everyone else? How many resources are already going into it? A big problem is more worth working on if it gets little attention and few resources already.

If few people work on a problem today, every extra hour, krone and person counts for more. If many are already working on it, the most obvious approaches have usually been tried.
{{< /value >}}
{{< value title="Tractable" >}}
Do we know that more resources in this area would actually make a noticeable difference? A problem can be big and neglected without being the right one to work on. Ask: how much of the problem could we expect to solve if we doubled the effort?

That does not mean only picking easy problems. But if you are choosing between two problems that are equally big and equally neglected, you will probably do most good where the opportunities for progress are best.
{{< /value >}}
{{< /values >}}

## Effective altruism in practice

### Global health and development

{{< stats >}}
{{< stat number="10M" label="deaths a year from diseases that can be prevented or treated" >}}
{{< stat number="$100" label="spent per person per year on healthcare in low-income countries" >}}
{{< stat number="$3,000–5,500" label="per life saved in GiveWell's top-rated programmes" >}}
{{< /stats >}}

Every year around 10 million people in poorer countries die of diseases that can be prevented or treated simply and cheaply – among them malaria, HIV, tuberculosis and diarrhoea.

Only about $100 per person per year is spent on healthcare in low-income countries, adjusted for purchasing power. There is still ample room to scale up treatments we know work.

One reason global health is considered among the world's most cost-effective areas for philanthropy is how little it takes to save or improve a life. Organisations like [GiveWell](https://www.givewell.org/charities/top-charities) identify interventions with documented impact per krone, often through randomised controlled trials. GiveWell estimates that its best programmes [save a life for $3,000–5,500](https://www.givewell.org/how-much-does-it-cost-to-save-a-life).

### Animal welfare

{{< stats >}}
{{< stat number="50bn" label="animals born and slaughtered each year in factory farming" >}}
{{< /stats >}}

Every year 50 billion animals are born and slaughtered in highly intensive food production. Many of them suffer greatly. Their cages and stalls are often so cramped that they can barely move, and they are bred for rapid growth under conditions that cause stress, injury and disease.

Even if animals do not carry the same moral weight as humans, there is good evidence that they are conscious and can feel pain. Despite the scale of the problem, the area is neglected. Possible solutions include advocacy to reduce demand for animal products, legislation improving conditions, and developing plant-based alternatives.

### Biorisk and pandemics

Some of the deadliest events in history have been pandemics. COVID-19 showed that we are still vulnerable, and future outbreaks could be far more dangerous. Technological progress makes it possible for biological disasters to be worse than ever.

The probability of such catastrophic pandemics is high enough that we count the risk among the world's most serious problems. There are several practical ways to reduce global catastrophic biological risks, which is why we think working on them is one of the most promising ways to protect humanity's future.

### AI safety

Preventing future AI systems from taking control away from humanity is, in our view, one of the most serious problems of our time to work on. The window for developing effective safety measures may be short. AI has enormous potential to improve human welfare, but also carries unprecedented dangers.

We think there are promising research directions and policy approaches that could make the difference between beneficial AI and not. Development has moved fast, powerful systems will likely arrive sooner than we assumed, and the risks are now discussed more widely.

## These are only a few examples
